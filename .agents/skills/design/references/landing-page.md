# Landing pages

A landing page has one job: move a specific visitor toward one action. Every section either advances that or gets cut. Read this when the brief is a landing page or marketing site; skip it for product UI.

## Decide the section order from the argument, not from a template

The generic order (hero → logos → three feature cards → testimonials → pricing → FAQ → CTA) is a default, not a design. Before laying out sections, write the page's argument as 3–5 plain sentences a visitor should believe in order, e.g.:

1. You have this problem and it's costing you.
2. Here's a different way to handle it.
3. It works — here's evidence.
4. It's easy to start.

Then give each sentence the section that proves it best. Some arguments need a demo before any features; some need proof first because the audience is skeptical; some need no pricing at all. The order follows the argument.

## Section-by-section standards

**Hero.** Follow frontend-design: open with the most characteristic thing in the subject's world. Additionally, the hero must contain the primary CTA and a headline a stranger could restate in their own words. Test: cover everything below the fold — does the visitor know what this is, who it's for, and what to do? If the answer requires the subheadline, tighten the headline.

**Proof.** Logos, numbers, testimonials, screenshots. Use whichever the client actually has; don't invent customers or statistics. If there's no proof yet, a concrete product demonstration is stronger than fake social proof. Placeholder proof must be marked as placeholder in a comment.

**Features / how it works.** Lead with the outcome for the user, then the mechanism. Three identical cards is the template answer; consider alternating image/text rows, a single annotated screenshot, a comparison, or a short walkthrough — whatever the content's real shape is.

**Objections.** A good landing page answers the top two or three reasons the visitor won't act — price, effort, trust, switching cost. This can be an FAQ, but it can also be a single reassuring line under the CTA or a comparison table.

**Final CTA.** Repeats the primary action with the same verb as the hero button. Add one line of low-friction reassurance (free, no card, cancel anytime — only if true).

## CTA discipline

- One primary action per page, one visual treatment for it, used in the hero and at the end at minimum.
- Secondary actions (docs, contact, demo video) get a visibly quieter treatment.
- Button text says what happens: "Start free trial", "Book a call", "Download the guide". Not "Get started", "Learn more", "Submit".
- Keep the CTA verb identical everywhere it appears.

## Length and pacing

Marketing pages sprawl. Aim for the shortest page that completes the argument. If a section exists only because "landing pages have one of those", cut it. Vary section rhythm — not every section is full-width, centered, with a heading on top.

## Pre-handoff check

- Headline passes the cover-the-fold test.
- Primary CTA visible in hero without scrolling at 360px and 1280px.
- Same CTA verb throughout.
- No invented proof.
- Section order maps to the written argument.
- Page works with images removed (placeholders describe what's missing).
